horde feedback lab

The four-agent survey (2026-08-15) produced hypotheses that only ears can settle, and feedback is the one area where being wrong is loud. This bench makes the loop audible with two things the survey said must exist and must be separate: a limiter as a safety backstop and an attenuator as the musical control — because "never let the thing that saves you be the thing you play". The whole loop runs in one AudioWorklet rather than a node graph, because the question this lab exists to answer is about delay granularity, and a node graph cannot express one sample.

Safety, by construction. Loop gain starts at zero — the universal practice from no-input mixing is to bring it up from silence until you can just hear it. A brickwall limiter is always on and cannot be disabled. An auto-kill fires if the pre-limiter signal stays hot for 400 ms, on the assumption that a runaway you have to reach for is a runaway that already hurt. The margin meter is the point of the bench. Practitioners describe playable feedback as a wide, controllable region near the threshold, not a stable system — so the thing worth designing is how much of the dial sits in that region. Watch where margin collapses as you raise loop gain, and whether the stabilisers below widen that region or merely move it.

The loop

This is OQ #23 made audible. The survey found the field splits by what travels on the edge: block-granular audio feedback draws complaints (Bitwig ~5.33 ms), control-rate cycle lag draws none (Csound). Sweep the granularity at a fixed loop gain and hear where it stops being a resonance and starts being an echo.

Stabilisers — do they widen the edge?

The frequency shift is the occluded one. Berdahl et al. (JASA 2012) measured ~3 Hz buying ≥3 dB of gain margin, because no frequency can re-enter the loop at exactly its own phase. Massive X ships a shifter inside its feedback loop and sells it as a tone colour. Test it directly: find the loop gain where it just sustains, then toggle the shift and see whether the margin meter moves. The lowpass is the DX7's two-sample average generalised — documented as anti-oscillation, not timbre.

Spring in the loop — testing our own correction

We filed route inertia with the caution that "a spring on a feedback edge is a second-order system inside a loop". The survey sharpened it into a claim: a spring with ζ < 1 does not smooth — it ADDS gain at its resonant frequency, so underdamped inertia on a feedback edge is a destabiliser. The falsifier is right here: set a loop gain that is stable at ζ = 1, then lower ζ. If the claim holds, the loop runs away without you touching the gain.
